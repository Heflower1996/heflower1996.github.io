Where my research is going

One question: how do markets and the people in them respond when conditions change?

Stream 1: Shocks, market adjustment, and resilience

How disease outbreaks, geopolitical events, trade disruptions, and policy changes affect prices, market access, and producer outcomes.

Builds on: machine learning forecasts of U.S. meat exports (LSTM and ANN against PPML), and the recovery of U.S. beef exports after the 2003 BSE outbreak.

Stream 2: New agricultural technologies

What drives adoption of precision technologies and AI, how producers value the information they provide, and how that changes production and management decisions.

Builds on: the adoption and economic value of AI in cattle ranching.

Stream 3: Consumer theory, willingness to pay, and food security

How buyers value product attributes and information, what they are willing to pay, and how food access and prices shape household food security.

Builds on: Mexican livestock producers’ willingness to pay for U.S. sorghum, and food deserts and local solutions in Texas.

Methods: econometrics, causal inference, machine learning.   Output: papers, Extension publications, grants.

Teaching philosophy

I guide students toward their goals and build critical thinking and independent problem solving.

Student focused

I check in with students every two weeks and adjust while the course is still running. In office hours I first ask how they learn best.

Real-world cases

Egg salmonella outbreak, toilet paper shortages, concert ticket prices, and my own sorghum research to teach demand and prices.

Story first

I open with a story students already care about, then connect it to the formal concept and let them apply it on their own.

AI, used responsibly

AI builds the what-if pricing scenarios. Students judge if the answer makes economic sense. In-class work carries more weight.

Case study: pricing a concert ticket

Ticket price:  
Tickets sold
Elasticity
Revenue
Illustrative demand: Q = 20,000 − 100P. The question for the class: the artist wants more revenue. Do we raise or lower the price?
Your turn. Elastic or inelastic?

How to solve it

1. Start from demand

Q = 20,000 − 100P

At a price of $60: Q = 20,000 − 100(60) = 14,000 tickets

2. Compute the elasticity

Elasticity = % change in tickets ÷ % change in price

Try it: raise the price 10%, from $60 to $66. Tickets fall 4.3%, from 14,000 to 13,400.

E = −4.3% ÷ 10% = −0.43

Tickets fall less than the price rises, so demand is inelastic. Revenue goes up, from $840,000 to $884,400. Raise the price.

3. Find the best price

Revenue = P × Q = 20,000P − 100P²

Revenue stops growing when 20,000 − 200P = 0, so P = $100

4. Check the answer

At $100: Q = 10,000, E = −1.00, revenue = $1,000,000

At $60 or at $140 revenue is only $840,000.

The rule: inelastic, raise the price. Elastic, lower the price. Revenue is highest where demand is unit elastic.

What do you think?

1. Substitutes. Another big artist plays in town the same weekend. What do you think happens to our ticket sales if we raise the price?

More substitutes make demand more elastic. Fans can switch to the other show, so a price increase loses more buyers and the best price is lower.

2. Time. Gasoline prices jump this week. Do you think people drive less this week? What about two years from now?

This week people still have to get to work, so demand is inelastic. With time they can change cars, routes, or jobs, so demand becomes more elastic.

3. Needs and wants. Why do you think a grocery store can raise the price of eggs more easily than the price of steak?

Eggs are a staple with few close substitutes and a small share of the budget, so demand is inelastic. Steak is easy to replace with chicken or pork, so demand is elastic.

4. The farmer. Every farmer has a great harvest and the price falls. Do you think farmers are better off?

Usually not. Demand for most farm products is inelastic, so the price falls by more than the quantity sold rises, and total revenue goes down.
