BSE Reopening and Market Share

Global Responses to Food-Safety Shocks: The Impact of Reopening BSE-Related Beef Import Bans on U.S. Exports

Henry Portillo, PhD Candidate

Advisor: Modhurima Amin, PhD

Department of Agricultural and Applied Economics

The question

  • In December 2003 the first U.S. BSE case led most major trading partners to restrict U.S. beef. U.S. beef exports fell from $2.8 billion a year (2000–03 average) to $0.5 billion in 2004.
  • Markets reopened one at a time, from 2004 (Mexico, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia) to 2019 (Australia), often under conditions.
  • Does reopening bring exports back? And does it bring back the U.S. share of what each market imports?
  • Fresh and frozen beef may respond differently, so we study them separately.

Research objective

How did U.S. beef exports change after foreign markets reopened, and how much of the U.S. market position came back?
  1. Estimate the export response to reopening for fresh and frozen beef, in the Americas and the rest of the world.
  2. Split the response into the extensive margin (does a market trade at all?) and the intensive margin (how much, once it trades?).
  3. Check the results with Synthetic DiD and placebo dates.
  4. Describe changes in market share, in the world market and in the main destinations.

Data

  • U.S. beef exports, UN Comtrade, annual, 1996–2019, in dollars and kilograms
  • Two products: HS 0201 fresh or chilled beef and HS 0202 frozen beef
  • 35 countries with a documented reopening after the ban; 33 no-reopening countries with no documented closure followed by a reopening
  • Missing flows are coded as zero. All 174 ambiguous zeros were checked against U.S. Census records and matched.
  • Reopening dates come from USDA, USTR and other official sources; nine markets rely on industry and trade sources.

When did markets reopen?

Treatment is the year of the first documented reopening, partial reopenings included. A December reopening is coded to the next year. Later closures (Japan, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia) are not modeled. Source: Table 3 (34 frozen and 32 fresh destinations in the estimation sample).

Empirical strategy

  • Callaway–Sant’Anna DiD. Built for staggered timing. Each reopening cohort is compared with markets that had not yet reopened and with the no-reopening countries.
  • Synthetic DiD. Robustness check. The counterfactual is a weighted mix of the no-reopening countries (33 for frozen, 16 for fresh), with weights on countries and on years.
  • Outcome is log(1 + export value). Event window runs from 10 years before to 5 years after reopening, and the sample ends in 2019.
  • Results are reported for all markets, the Americas, and the rest of the world. Margins are estimated separately.

Main result: fresh beef responds, frozen beef does not

Fresh beef exports rose by 4.09 log points (95% CI 1.79 to 6.38) under Callaway–Sant’Anna and 4.74 (2.45 to 7.03) under SDID. For frozen beef both intervals include zero.

Fresh beef jumps at reopening

Callaway–Sant’Anna event-study estimates. Event time 0 is the first documented reopening year. Pre-reopening coefficients sit around zero.

Two margins, two different stories

  • Fresh: reopening raised the chance that a market imports U.S. fresh beef by about 28 percentage points. Shipments to markets already buying did not change detectably.
  • Frozen: no change in whether markets trade, but exports to markets that trade were about 240 percent higher (CI roughly 40 to 724 percent).

The U.S. share of world beef exports

The U.S. share fell from 19.5% to 2.8% in 2004, when the United States dropped from first to eleventh. By 2019 it was 13.3%. Brazil and India took most of the 6.2 points that did not come back.

Who filled the gap?

In Japan and South Korea, Australia took over in 2004 and kept part of the market. In Hong Kong, South America took the lead and is still ahead of the United States. Supplier shares are importer-reported, so U.S. values differ slightly from the map.

What it means

  • Reopening restored U.S. sales: exports are about two and a half times their pre-ban dollar level.
  • It only partly restored the U.S. position. The combined U.S. share of Japan, South Korea and Hong Kong peaked at 51.0% in 2018, against 58.5% before the ban.
  • The reopening date matters. The frozen beef result changes from significant to not significant when dates move from industry reports to first documented reopening.
  • Success after an animal-disease shock should be judged by whether the exporter regains its place in the market, not only by whether markets formally reopen.

What it doesn’t mean

  • Treatment is the first documented reopening. It is not full liberalization, and partial reopenings carry conditions we cannot separate across countries.
  • The frozen beef result is an absence of evidence, not evidence that reopening did not matter: the intensive margin is significant.
  • The market share evidence is descriptive. It estimates no treatment effect and ends in 2019.
  • Importer totals and U.S. records are valued differently, Taiwan has no importer series, and Hong Kong re-exports part of its imports.
